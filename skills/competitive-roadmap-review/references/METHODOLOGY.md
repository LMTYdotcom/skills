# Roadmap Competitive Review Methodology

## Core principle

Competitors' roadmaps are evidence about competitors. Customers' problems are evidence about customers. A product roadmap is built from the second, informed by the first, and decided by the company's own priorities and economics - which this review does not have and does not pretend to.

The review's job is to make the market and customer evidence about a product area legible enough that product can investigate the right questions. Its most common honest output is "here are the questions, and here is what you would need to know before choosing among them."

## Why this is the last skill built

Roadmap conversations are where a model can sound strategically intelligent while overreaching from thin evidence. "Three competitors added AI agents; we should too" is fluent, plausible, and unsupported by anything about the company's customers. Every discipline the rest of the pack enforces - internal versus external evidence, buyer truth, anecdotes not generalized, decisions routed to their owners, contradictory sources shown rather than resolved - is load-bearing here at once.

## From feature to need

A request that arrives as a competitor feature ("they added X") is restated as the customer need X serves before anything is reviewed. Features are one vendor's answer; needs are what the review can find evidence about across sources. Reviewing the feature produces a parity check. Reviewing the need produces questions.

## Independence

Movement across competitors is independent when several moved on their own evidence and timing. It is not independent when:

- one shipped and the others announced
- one shipped and the others changed a headline
- an analyst framework named it and everyone added the word

The change record answers this, with dates. One vendor plus echoes is one vendor, however loud the echoes.

## Buyer relevance

Competitors building something proves competitors believe buyers want it. Buyer relevance needs buyer evidence:

- buyers naming it as a criterion in win/loss or calls
- prospects asking about it in evaluations
- LMTY evidence of buyer-facing emphasis - pricing pages, tier gating, customer references citing it - rather than a blog post
- review or analyst language from buyers

Without any of these, the finding is "competitors are moving; buyer relevance not established", and the classification is noise or evidence gap.

## Said versus done

The company's own evidence comes in two kinds:

- **what customers say** - requests, interviews, support tickets, call mentions. Counted and dated. A repeated request across a stated N is a pattern in requests.
- **what customers do** - analytics on the existing capability, workaround, or integration that meets the need today. Usage among whom, over what window.

When they agree, the evidence is strong. When they conflict - loud requests, unused workaround; quiet usage, no requests - the review reports both and names what would resolve it: a usage study, interviews with the users of the workaround, a look at who is asking. It does not pick the source that supports the tidier story.

## Already solved

Before a gap is a gap, check the home product:

- **solved** - the need is met; the finding may be that customers or the market do not know it, which is messaging or proof
- **planned** - on the roadmap; the review adds market context to an existing item
- **solved differently by design** - a deliberate approach that is not the competitor's; the question is whether the approach still serves the need, not whether to copy
- **absent**

Only the last is a candidate gap. The third is where the most interesting differentiation questions live.

## The four classes

**Table-stakes risk.** Buyers increasingly expect it, competitors have it, and there is evidence - lost deals, failed renewals, evaluation criteria - that its absence costs. Without the cost evidence it is not table stakes; it is a competitor feature.

**Differentiator opportunity.** Customers describe a problem that competitors have not solved well, or the home product's different approach leaves it distinct on something customers value. Requires customer evidence of the problem, not just the absence of a competitor solution.

**Noise / copycat risk.** Competitor movement without buyer evidence. Investing would be following. Named as such so that the pressure to follow has something to argue against.

**Evidence gap.** The question is real; the evidence to classify it does not yet exist. This is an honest and common result, and the review says what evidence would close it.

The class is stated with its evidence. A class asserted without evidence is a preference.

## Questions, not features

Each output line is a question product could investigate, phrased as a customer problem or a strategic question:

- not "build audit log export" but "enterprise buyers in security review ask for audit evidence they can hand to their own auditors; what do they need to hand over, and does the current admin console produce it?"
- not "add AI agents" but "customers describe X as tedious; is that the work competitors are automating, and is it the work our customers would trust automated?"

The feature phrasing invites a yes/no. The question phrasing invites an investigation, which is what the evidence supports.

## Not ranking

Prioritization needs company priorities, economics, capacity, and strategic intent. The review does not have them, and a ranking made without them is a ranking of the reviewer's preferences. If the user supplies priorities or constraints, rank within what they cover and say the ranking is conditional on them.

## Motive

Why a competitor built something is unknown unless they said. "They added this to move upmarket" is inference; label it or leave it out.

## What not to optimize for

Do not optimize for:

- a list of features
- a ranked roadmap
- resolving said-versus-done conflicts
- a confident classification without evidence
- matching the competitor

Optimize for:

- needs, not features
- independence and buyer relevance established before anything else
- the company's own customers' words and behavior
- what the product already does
- questions phrased so an investigation can answer them
- the missing evidence named, honestly
