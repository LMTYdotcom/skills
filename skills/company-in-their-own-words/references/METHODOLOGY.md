# In Their Own Words Methodology

## Why verbatim

Messaging work fails when it argues with a summary. "They position as an enterprise platform" hides whether the page says "platform", "suite" or "system", and whether "enterprise" is in the headline or a footer link. The words are the evidence; collect them before anyone interprets them.

## What counts as the company's own words

| Counts | Does not count |
| --- | --- |
| Headings and descriptions on its own pages | An analyst's description of it |
| The paragraph its press releases end with | A review site's category for it |
| Its bios on its own accounts | A journalist's paraphrase |
| What its founders and executives say in public | What an employee without a leadership role says in passing |
| Its earnings calls and keynotes | A competitor's comparison page about it |
| A direct quote from it in the press | The headline the press wrote |

When something from the right-hand column is all there is, leave the section empty and say so.

## The four layers

Collect each layer separately. They are written by different people for different readers and often disagree.

1. **The headline layer:** the main heading and the line under it, per page
2. **The category layer:** the noun phrase it uses for what it is
3. **The proof layer:** the numbers and names it cites
4. **The spoken layer:** what its leaders say unscripted

## Describing a change without interpreting it

Pair the old wording and the new, then describe only the difference in words:

| Write | Not |
| --- | --- |
| "The homepage heading changed from 'Invoicing for small teams' to 'The finance platform for growing companies' between 2024-06-01 and 2024-11-14." | "They pivoted to platform." |
| "'For small teams' no longer appears in the heading." | "They are moving upmarket." |
| "The proof line changed from a customer count to a share of large companies." | "They are chasing enterprise." |
| "'Platform' first appears on the homepage in an archived copy dated 2024-10-01." | "They are following the market." |

Kinds of change worth naming: a term added, a term dropped, the audience named differently, the category noun replaced, the proof swapped for another kind, the same message moved to a more or less prominent place.

## Dates of wording

- **last seen:** the most recent read that still showed it. The date for "current"
- **first seen:** when it was first recorded. Often the first day the page was read, which says nothing about when it was written
- **ended:** the wording was gone on a later read
- **earliest use of a term:** may come from an archived copy and can predate everything else in the record

Only "ended" and a dated archive copy support "changed". Never date a rewrite to the day the pages were first read.

A leader saying later how the company used to describe itself is a recollection. It carries the date it was said, which is the date of the recording or publication, and it is quoted as a recollection: "in a talk dated 2024-10, its CEO recalled describing it as 'invoicing for small teams'". It is not evidence of that wording on any earlier date, and it never fills a "before" cell.

## Proof points

Record, do not rate. For each: the exact wording, what it counts, the page, the date.

Useful to note, because it shapes what a reader can do with it:

- whether the number has a unit and a base ("four of the ten largest banks" names its base; "trusted by thousands" does not)
- whether it is dated or sourced on the page
- whether the same figure appears elsewhere with a different value

Testing whether it is true is another skill's job.

## Spoken statements

- choose statements that carry information: a number, a plan, a view of a competitor, a description of the customer
- quote the sentence, not the paragraph. One to three lines
- give speaker and role, venue, date, and where in the recording when the profile has it
- keep the profile's grade. Official means the speaker spoke for a company, which can be another company: check their employer. Anything else is their remark
- a remark that names no company (a view on the market, on hiring, on technology) is the speaker's own. It goes under their name and role, and is the company's position only when it is about the company's own product, customers or plans, or the speaker says they speak for it
- a statement by another company's leader about this company is not this company's words. It is left out, and the record says in one line how many such statements the profile holds
- transcripts from automatic captions drop words and mishear names. Keep the transcript's words, add "[sic]" only where a reader would stumble, and say once that the quotes are from captions

A spoken number ("we're about 300 people now") is a statement by a leader on a date. Report it as that, not as the company's headcount.

## When the subject is a person

A request about one founder or executive is answered from that person's profile, and the spoken layer is the whole record.

- start from the person's profile; the current roles on it say which company they speak for, if any
- collect their recorded statements, each with venue, date and grade, newest first, under the rules above
- a remark that names no company is their own view. It is their company's position only when it is about that company's own product, customers or plans, or they say they speak for it
- what someone else said about the person is not their words. It is left out, and the record says in one line how many such statements the profile holds
- what the person says about another company is theirs. It is never that company's own words
- say which company they speak for, and that the company's own wording is a separate read of its profile
- when the profile lists what they wrote or where they appeared, give those as venues with dates. A title is not a quote

The headline, category and proof layers do not exist for a person. Leave those sections out; do not print them empty or fill them from the company's pages.

## Comparing the voices

Use a small grid: rows for the pages, the boilerplate and the leaders; columns for what it is, who it is for, what it is an alternative to, and where it is going. Fill cells with quotes or "not stated".

Then one or two sentences on where rows differ. Differences are normal. An executive describing next year's company while the website describes this year's is the commonest one.

## Length

The current wording and the grid fit on a page. The quotes can run longer; put them last, newest first, and cap them at ten unless asked for all.
