# Competitive Battlecard Methodology

## Battlecard design principle

Battlecards are often requested and often ignored. The skill should optimize for the moment of use, not for completeness.

A useful card combines:
- current market truth
- customer/deal truth
- short language a rep can say
- discovery that exposes fit
- honest qualification guidance

## Core card anatomy

1. Thirty-second competitor orientation
2. When we see them
3. Why customers choose them, and who says so
4. Three primary differentiation themes
5. Discovery questions that expose trade-offs
6. Objections from buyers' own words, and response posture
7. Proof and customer examples
8. Where they are stronger / when we may lose
9. Recent changes to know, each dated by how the date is known
10. What was read, and what the card lacks

## "Quick dismiss" caution

Traditional battlecards often include a dismissive one-liner. Avoid cheap shots. Prefer a fair framing that redirects the buyer toward decision criteria where the home product has a real advantage.

## Customer evidence hierarchy

Strongest for field guidance:
1. repeated win/loss pattern
2. repeated call or CRM pattern
3. validated switch story
4. individual deal example
5. PMM hypothesis based on public differences

The last category can be useful, but label it as such. The card's "Basis" column carries this label for every theme and objection.

Public reviews are not on this list. A review is third-party opinion, not deal evidence. It supplies wording, labeled as "Public reviews" in `references/TRUST.md` says: the words reviewers use for what they praise and complain about. A criterion or a question worded from a review keeps the basis "assumed, not from buyers" or "hypothesis", and a complaint in a review is not an objection a buyer raised in a deal.

## When deal evidence is missing

The strongest pull in a card is to fill the objections table. With no calls, CRM or win/loss, the table is left out and one line says what is missing and which source would fill it. An "anticipated" objection reads as a real one once the card is forwarded, and a rep will answer it in front of a buyer who never raised it.

The same holds for "why buyers choose them": with no deal evidence, write what the competitor's own pages lead with, attributed, and say it is not known from deals.

## Absence and dates

A capability, plan or certification not found is "not observed in the sources checked, as of [date]". A card is where an absence turns into "they don't have it" in a rep's mouth, so no theme or question rests on one.

A change on the card carries the date its source gives for the change itself. A date that is only when LMTY recorded or first saw something is written "recorded on" or "seen on", and the change is not called new.

## Conversation design

A rep usually needs:
- one framing sentence
- one or two discovery questions
- one proof point

The deep detail belongs behind the live layer.

## When to walk away

A high-trust battlecard tells sales when not to force a fit.

Examples:
- buyer requires a capability the home product does not have
- segment economics make the competitor structurally better
- switching cost overwhelms the available value

This is a feature, not a weakness, of the battlecard.
