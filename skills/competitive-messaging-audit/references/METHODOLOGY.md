# Messaging Review Methodology

## Core principle

Messaging is the words chosen to carry a position to an audience. A review tests the words against three things the team does not control - what competitors say, what customers say, what the market did - and one thing it does: the position it decided on.

The review does not decide the position. When the words fail because the position is wrong, the review says so and stops.

## The hierarchy

Most messages, whatever their format, do five jobs in roughly this order:

1. **Category** - what kind of thing this is, for a buyer who may not know
2. **Value** - what changes for the buyer
3. **Differentiation** - why this rather than the alternative
4. **Proof** - why believe it
5. **Call to action** - what to do next

The first diagnosis is structural: which jobs the message does, in what proportion, for which audience. Common structural findings:

- differentiation without proof
- category explanation for buyers who know the category
- value stated in the product's terms rather than the buyer's outcome
- three differentiators competing for the lead
- a CTA that assumes a decision the message has not earned

## Three tests for every material claim

**Distinctness.** Does any tracked competitor say this? LMTY's positioning data for each competitor, with its change record, answers when they started. A claim nobody else makes is distinct. A claim three competitors make is generic - and the change record says whether the home product had it first, which matters for what to do about it.

**Value.** Do customers care? Calls, interviews, reviews, win/loss: do buyers mention this dimension when they describe the problem or the decision? A claim no customer mentions is either ahead of the market or irrelevant, and the review should not guess which.

**Proof.** Can it be backed? Approved product facts, documentation, references, measurements. A claim customers value that nothing backs is the most urgent gap, because it is the one the buyer will test.

A claim can pass all three, or any subset:

| Distinct | Valued | Proven | Sort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yes | yes | yes | keep |
| yes | no | - | differentiated but irrelevant |
| no | yes | yes | generic - sharpen proof or find a new angle |
| yes | yes | no | valuable but unproven |
| no | no | - | true but not valuable |

## Convergence is about words

When competitors start using the home product's language, three things may be true:

- **the products converged too** - real parity; the claim should be demoted
- **the products did not** - the message needs proof sharp enough to separate them, not a new adjective
- **the products were never different** - the claim was parity dressed as difference from the start

The review checks the products - current evidence, both sides - before calling anything parity. "Everyone says AI-native now" is a finding about copy. Whether the home product's AI differs is a separate question with a separate answer.

## Customer vocabulary

The single most useful thing the review can put in front of a team is a two-column table: what the message says, what customers say. It comes from customers - transcripts, interviews, reviews, win/loss quotes - never from the team's memory of what customers say, which is usually the message played back.

Look for:

- the message's nouns that customers never use
- the customers' nouns the message never uses
- the criteria customers decide on that the message does not address
- the claim the message leads with that no customer raised

## Performance data and interviews

When a page test says one message wins and interviews say customers talk differently, both are evidence. The test measured a behavior - a click, a form fill - among whoever saw that page. The interviews recorded language among whoever was interviewed. Neither is the truth about the message. Report both, say what each measured, and let the team decide which behavior they are optimizing.

## Preserving the position

The position is the team's decision: who it is for, against what alternative, what is different, why believe it. The review takes it as given and tests whether the words carry it.

When evidence undermines the position - customers are a different audience, the alternative changed, the differentiator has gone - the honest finding is "this is not a messaging problem". Say it, route it to `competitive-positioning`, and do not fix it with copy. Repositioning through a copy review produces a message with no decision behind it.

## Recommendations

A recommendation carries:

- the change
- the evidence
- confidence, honestly: strong when customer data and competitor data agree; moderate when one source; weak when inferred
- suggested wording only when the change is about words

Changes that are about proof say "get or state the proof"; changes that are about position say "decide". Writing new copy for either is writing over the problem.

## Bounded input

A copy review with only the copy supplied can test structure and internal consistency. It cannot test distinctness or customer fit, and the review must not fill those from memory - the reviewer's sense of "what competitors say" is exactly the folklore the review exists to replace. Say which tests could not run and what would run them.

## What not to optimize for

Do not optimize for:

- a rewrite
- novelty of claims
- agreement with the team's read of customers
- a verdict on parity from words alone

Optimize for:

- structure named before claims
- each claim tested three ways, with evidence
- customers' words beside the message's
- the position kept out of the copy
- confidence stated honestly
