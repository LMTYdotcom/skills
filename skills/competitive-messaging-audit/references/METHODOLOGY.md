# Competitive Messaging Audit Methodology

## Core principle

Messaging is the words chosen to carry a position to an audience. An audit tests the words against three things the team does not control - what competitors say, what customers say, what the market did - and one thing it does: the position it decided on.

The audit does not decide the position. When the words fail because the position is wrong, the audit says so and stops.

## The hierarchy

Most messages, whatever their format, do five jobs in roughly this order:

1. **Category** - what kind of thing this is, for a buyer who may not know
2. **Value** - what changes for the buyer
3. **Differentiation** - why this rather than the alternative
4. **Proof** - why believe it
5. **Call to action** - what to do next

The first diagnosis is structural: which jobs the message does, in what proportion, for which audience. Common structural findings:

- differentiation without proof
- category explanation for buyers who know the category
- value stated in the product's terms rather than the buyer's outcome
- three differentiators competing for the lead
- a CTA that assumes a decision the message has not earned

## Three tests for every material claim

**Distinctness.** Does any competitor read say this? The report's positioning section and each competitor's public profile (its headings, taglines, category claim, proof points and boilerplate, verbatim with page and date) answer what they say now. A claim none of the competitors read makes is distinct as of their dates, for those competitors: say how many of the tracked set that is. A claim three competitors make is generic.

When they started is known only from a date of the wording's own: an archived page, a dated launch, the earliest known use of a category term, the end date of the wording it replaced. A change record entry is dated by when LMTY recorded it, and on a young account every entry is a first sighting: write "on record since [date]; when they began is not known". Never say who said it first from the order in which LMTY recorded the two.

**Value.** Do customers care? Calls, interviews, win/loss: do buyers mention this dimension when they describe the problem or the decision? Public reviews, labeled, show whether reviewers mention it. They never show what buyers decide on. A claim no customer mentions in the conversations read is either ahead of the market or irrelevant, and the audit should not guess which. With no customer evidence at all, value is not tested: the sort is "untested", never "irrelevant". Say how many were read.

**Proof.** Can it be backed? Approved product facts, documentation, references, measurements. A claim customers value that nothing backs is the most urgent gap, because it is the one the buyer will test.

A claim can pass all three, or any subset:

| Distinct | Valued | Proven | Sort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yes | yes | yes | keep |
| yes | no | - | differentiated but irrelevant |
| yes | not tested | - | untested: no customer evidence either way |
| no | yes | yes | generic - sharpen proof or find a new angle |
| yes | yes | no | valuable but unproven |
| no | no | - | true but not valuable |

## Convergence is about words

When competitors start using the home product's language, three things may be true:

- **the products converged too** - real parity; the claim should be demoted
- **the products did not** - the message needs proof sharp enough to separate them, not a new adjective
- **the products were never different** - the claim was parity dressed as difference from the start

The audit checks the products - current evidence, both sides - before calling anything parity. "Everyone says AI-native now" is a finding about copy. Whether the home product's AI differs is a separate question with a separate answer. A competitor capability known only from its own pages is its claim: "unknown: their own claim only", unless independent evidence or hands-on use is in front of you.

## Customer vocabulary

The single most useful thing the audit can put in front of a team is a two-column table: what the message says, what customers say. It comes from customers - transcripts, interviews, win/loss quotes, and public reviews labeled as third-party opinion - never from the team's memory of what customers say, which is usually the message played back.

Look for:

- the message's nouns that customers never use
- the customers' nouns the message never uses
- the criteria customers decide on that the message does not address
- the claim the message leads with that no customer raised

Public reviews of the home product or of a competitor are the second source, under "Public reviews" in `references/TRUST.md`. They are read on the review site's page in this run, never recalled. Each row that quotes one carries its label in the Source column: third-party opinion, the site, the review's date, one reviewer's account. Say how many reviews were read, on which sites and between which dates. Three cases:

- **The company's own evidence and reviews.** The company's rows lead. Review rows sit beside them, marked, and are never merged into a count of calls or interviews.
- **Reviews only.** The user gave no customer evidence and reviews can be read. Build the table from the reviews, and say above it that the customer side rests on public reviews alone. Reviews show words, praise, complaints and how reviewers name the category. They do not show what buyers decide on: that line reads "not known: reviews do not show it". A claim sorted on reviews alone says so in its evidence, with how many reviews.
- **Neither.** The test is not run. Leave the table out, say so in one line, and name what would run it.

A review a competitor quotes on its own site is that competitor's selection and is labeled as the vendor's. One review is one voice: never "customers say", never a share of anyone.

Recorded public posts are a different kind of evidence, read only when the user asks or the question clearly turns on them. An author is a handle, not a customer or a buyer, unless the post says so. Their words go in a section of their own, sorted by who is speaking, never into this table as what customers say, and never summarized below the floor in `references/SOCIAL.md`: under 20 posts by 10 authors, the posts are listed, not summarized.

## Performance data and interviews

When a page test says one message wins and interviews say customers talk differently, both are evidence. The test measured a behavior - a click, a form fill - among whoever saw that page. The interviews recorded language among whoever was interviewed. Neither is the truth about the message. Report both, say what each measured, and let the team decide which behavior they are optimizing.

## Preserving the position

The position is the team's decision: who it is for, against what alternative, what is different, why believe it. The audit takes it as given and tests whether the words carry it.

When evidence undermines the position - customers are a different audience, the alternative changed, the differentiator has gone - the honest finding is "this is not a messaging problem". Say it, route it to `competitive-positioning`, and do not fix it with copy. Repositioning through a copy audit produces a message with no decision behind it.

## Recommendations

A recommendation carries:

- the change
- the evidence
- confidence, honestly: strong when customer data and competitor data agree; moderate when one source; weak when inferred. Public reviews alone are not customer data for this: moderate at most
- suggested wording only when the change is about words

Changes that are about proof say "get or state the proof"; changes that are about position say "decide". Writing new copy for either is writing over the problem. Every recommendation is the auditor's judgment and is labeled so; a contradicted claim names the source that contradicts it, and whether to remove it is the owner's call.

## Bounded input

A copy audit with only the copy supplied can test structure and internal consistency. It cannot test distinctness or customer fit, and the audit must not fill those from memory - the auditor's sense of "what competitors say" is exactly the folklore the audit exists to replace. Say which tests could not run and what would run them.

## What not to optimize for

Do not optimize for:

- a rewrite
- novelty of claims
- agreement with the team's read of customers
- a verdict on parity from words alone

Optimize for:

- structure named before claims
- each claim tested three ways, with evidence
- customers' words beside the message's
- the position kept out of the copy
- confidence stated honestly
