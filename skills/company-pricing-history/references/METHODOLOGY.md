# Pricing History Methodology

## What a price record can and cannot say

A profile's price record is a series of readings of public pages. It can say what was listed on each date. It cannot say what a customer paid, what a sales team quoted, or what the company intended.

Three things follow:

- the record has a first day, and nothing before it is known
- a change is dated by when it was noticed, unless a source states when it was announced or took effect
- silence between two readings is "no change observed", which is weaker than "no change"

## Kinds of move

Name each move with one of these, so a reader can count them:

| Move | Means |
| --- | --- |
| New plan | A plan appears that the page did not list before |
| Price up / price down | The same plan, a different listed figure |
| Plan retired | No longer listed at all |
| Closed to new buyers | Still honored for existing customers, no longer sold |
| Allowance changed | Same price, different limits, seats, credits or usage |
| Model changed | Per seat to usage, monthly to annual only, flat to tiered |
| Renamed | Same price and contents, new name |

A rename with a price change is two moves. An allowance cut at the same price is a price rise for anyone who used the allowance; say that as an observation about the allowance, not as "raised prices".

## Writing a move

- "Pro went from $20 to $30 a seat a month. First seen on the pricing page 2024-11-14."
- "The free plan is no longer listed as of 2024-10-27. No announcement observed."
- "Team was closed to new subscribers on 2024-11-14, per the company's own post; existing subscribers keep it."

Keep the listed unit. "$240 a year" and "$20 a month" are different facts when the page offers only one of them.

## Patterns worth stating

State a pattern only when the record supports it with a count:

- cadence: "four moves in eleven months, the last three within ten weeks"
- direction: "the entry price fell twice; the top plan rose once"
- where the change lands: "three of the four moves changed allowances, not prices"
- shape of the list: "from three plans to seven between March and November"
- treatment of existing customers, when stated: "both rises applied to new subscribers only"

## Patterns not to claim

| Do not write | Because |
| --- | --- |
| "They raise prices every year." | Two rises a year apart are two rises |
| "Pricing has been stable." | The record may be three weeks long |
| "They are moving upmarket." | That is a strategy, inferred from a price list |
| "The free tier was removed to drive conversions." | Motive, with no statement behind it |
| "Customers are paying about $40." | One reported figure is one person's account |
| "Prices will rise again in Q1." | A prediction |

## Reported prices

A reported price is somebody saying what they pay or were quoted. It is useful for one thing: showing that the realized price may differ from the list, and in which direction people say it does.

- give the figure, the plan if stated, who said it, where and when
- keep the grade: one account is unverified, however specific it is
- several accounts that agree are still accounts. Count them; do not promote them to a price
- reported prices sit in two places: the facts of that kind on the profile, and its public posts, which are unverified and read only when those facts are empty or the user asks. Say which was read, with counts, before writing that the profile has none

## Currency and region

Write the currency the page showed. If the profile holds prices in more than one currency or region, list them side by side with their dates. Do not convert.

## When the record is thin

A product read for the first time last week has a price list and no history. The useful output is short: the list, the date, and one sentence saying the record starts now. Offer nothing about the past.
