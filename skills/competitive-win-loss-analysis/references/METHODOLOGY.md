# Win/Loss Analysis Methodology

## Core principle

The reason a deal was won or lost is what the buyer says it was, weighed against what the buyer did. Everything else - the rep's field, the manager's theory, the pattern everyone "knows" - is a hypothesis about the buyer.

The analysis exists to replace folklore with counts, and to say honestly how much the counts can bear.

## Two columns, never merged

Every deal has up to two accounts of why:

- **Buyer-stated**: interview quotes, the decision call, an email explaining the choice. Direct evidence, from the person who decided.
- **Rep-entered**: the loss reason field, the risk reason, the notes. Evidence of what the team believed, written by someone with a stake in the explanation.

They are kept in separate columns for the whole analysis. Where they agree, the finding is stronger. Where they disagree, the disagreement is the finding - it usually reveals what the field is teaching itself that buyers do not confirm.

"Price" is the canonical case. It is the most frequent rep-entered loss reason in most CRMs, and buyer evidence in the same deals most often says familiarity, incumbency, a missed requirement, or a security review that never finished. Check every price loss against the buyer column.

## Counting

A pattern is: *this criterion or theme appears in N of the M deals that have buyer evidence*, followed by the deals where it does not appear or runs the other way.

Rules:

- the denominator is deals with buyer evidence, not all deals
- record-only deals are reported separately and do not vote
- a theme mentioned once is a single observation, whatever its vividness
- exceptions are listed next to the pattern, not in a footnote
- when the buyer gave an order ("first speed, then integrations, then price"), keep it; ordering is data

Three deals are three observations. Six deals with buyer evidence can show that something recurs; they cannot show that it is the reason. Say which the sample supports.

## Segmentation

Segment only when each bucket can carry a finding on its own. A reasonable floor is four or five deals with buyer evidence per bucket, and it should be stated. Below the floor, report the deals individually and do not name a segment pattern.

The temptation is to segment until a story appears. Every additional split is another chance for noise to look like signal.

## Market changes and timing

LMTY's change record and the home product's own pricing and product changes are context for the window. They become part of the explanation of a deal only when:

1. the change happened before that deal decided, and
2. the buyer evidence for that deal mentions the change or its effect.

Without (1) the change is irrelevant to that deal. Without (2) it is a hypothesis. A competitor's September price cut cannot explain a June loss, and a plausible mechanism is not a buyer saying so.

## Bias

Every deal evidence set is biased, and the report says how:

- **who was interviewed**: losses only, or wins too; large deals only; deals a rep chose to flag
- **who interviewed**: the vendor, whose buyers soften; a third party, whose buyers are franker
- **who answered**: the champion, who was on the home product's side; the decision-maker, who was not necessarily
- **what was recorded**: CRM competitor fields are filled unevenly; unfilled is not "no competitor"
- **when**: interviews weeks after the decision are memories of a decision, reshaped

None of this disqualifies the analysis. All of it bounds what it can claim.

## Locating the cause

Buyer evidence usually distributes across a few familiar causes. For each - product, positioning, sales execution, price, implementation and switching, trust and compliance, incumbency - the report shows where the buyer column puts weight and where the rep column does. The columns disagree most often on:

- price (rep) versus familiarity or switching cost (buyer)
- product gap (rep) versus a requirement the team never surfaced (buyer)
- "they lied about their product" (rep) versus the buyer's actual criteria (buyer)

## Hypotheses instead of causes

The analysis ends with hypotheses because that is the honest resolution of the evidence. Each hypothesis carries:

- the evidence for it, counted
- the evidence against it, or missing
- the test that would settle it: more interviews in a segment, a discovery question added and tracked, a product or pricing check, a review of the security process

A hypothesis with no test is a belief.

## Privacy in the artifact

Win/loss reports travel further than most PMM artifacts. Quotes are attributed by role and outcome, not name, unless the audience already has access to the source. Contract values appear only where the analysis needs them. One customer's private situation is not generalized into a claim about customers.

## What not to optimize for

Do not optimize for:

- a single clean reason
- confirming the field's existing theory
- a chart that looks like statistics from eight deals
- causes over hypotheses

Optimize for:

- buyer words over rep words
- counts with their exceptions
- a bias statement the reader can check
- timing that holds before a market change is blamed
- hypotheses with tests
