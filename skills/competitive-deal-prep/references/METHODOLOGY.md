# Competitive Deal Prep Methodology

## Core principle

Preparation is knowing what is established, what is assumed, and what is unknown about one deal - and walking into the meeting to convert the second and third into the first.

A prep document that reads as a plan to win has usually done the opposite: it has filled the unknowns with the rep's hopes and the competitor's reputation.

## The four layers of a deal

Work from the most reliable to the least:

1. **Recorded state** - stage, value, dates, stakeholders as captured. Reliable for what it is; stale as of its last update.
2. **What the buyer said** - calls, meeting notes, emails. The only direct evidence of priorities and criteria. Quote it.
3. **What the market shows** - current competitor state, evidence, recent changes. Reliable for the competitor's public posture; silent on what this buyer knows or believes.
4. **What the team thinks** - rep notes, risk reasons, channel chatter, the user's framing. Necessary context, and the layer most likely to be wrong. Label it.

A prep that mixes layer 4 into layer 2 is the most common failure: "the buyer is price-sensitive" when the buyer said security and the rep wrote price.

## Who is in the deal

A competitor is in the deal when:

- the buyer named it
- the buyer's requirements or vocabulary come from it (a criterion phrased in a competitor's marketing language is evidence)
- the opportunity record names it, on a stated date
- the buyer is currently using it (the incumbent is always in the deal)

A competitor is *not* in the deal because it is the market leader, because it usually is, or because the rep expects it. List assumed competitors separately.

When several are active, the prep covers all of them against the confirmed criteria. Picking one to focus on is the team's decision, made with the picture in front of them.

## Confirmed versus assumed criteria

For each candidate criterion ask: can I point to a buyer statement? If yes, it is confirmed, with the who and when. If not, it is assumed, with its origin.

Common assumptions dressed as criteria:

- the CRM risk reason ("price")
- the rep's read of the champion's mood
- what "buyers in this segment" care about
- the competitor's own positioning, taken as what the buyer wants

Assumptions are not worthless. They are the questions for the next meeting.

## The three questions

Discovery questions in general are for the objection skill and the battlecard. Deal prep asks a harder thing: of everything unknown, which three unknowns most change what the team should do?

Usually one from each:

- **Requirement hardness** - is the criterion the competitor wins on mandatory or preferred?
- **Decision mechanics** - who decides, by when, against what written or unwritten criteria?
- **The buyer's current belief** - what have they already concluded about a competitor, from what source, and how recently?

If the prep cannot name three, the deal is either very well understood or the prep has not looked hard enough. It is usually the second.

## Proof selection

Proof is chosen against confirmed criteria, from approved sources, in this order:

1. documentation that directly answers a stated requirement
2. an approved customer reference matching segment or concern
3. a measured outcome relevant to a stated priority
4. a demonstration the team can run in the meeting

A criterion with no matching approved proof is listed as such. That is useful: it tells the team to get proof or to stop leaning on the claim.

## Risks

A prep names risk when:

- a confirmed criterion favors a competitor
- a stakeholder who matters has not been heard
- the record is stale, or the champion has gone quiet
- the plan rests on an assumption that the next meeting could overturn
- the requirement set suggests the home product is not the right fit

The last one is the hardest to write and the most valuable. A team that walks into a poor-fit deal prepared to win has been prepared badly.

## Recent competitor changes

Include a change only when this account would plausibly notice or be affected: a price change on the tier they are evaluating, a certification added that their security review asks about, an integration shipped that the buyer named. General competitor news is a briefing, not a prep.

## What travels

The prep will be forwarded. Write it so it can be:

- account detail limited to what the team needs for this meeting
- no other customer's private situation used as proof
- no line that would embarrass the company if the buyer saw it

## What not to optimize for

Do not optimize for:

- confidence
- completeness of the competitor's weaknesses
- a scripted path to close
- volume of discovery questions

Optimize for:

- the buyer's words over everyone's opinion of them
- a clean split between established, assumed and unknown
- three questions that matter
- proof that fits
- a risks section the team would rather not read
