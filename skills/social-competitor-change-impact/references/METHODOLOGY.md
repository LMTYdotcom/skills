# Competitor Change Impact Methodology

## Three things that get blurred

| | What it is | Where it comes from | How it is written |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Possible impact | What a change of this kind could do | Reasoning from the change | "could", in its own column |
| Reported experience | What someone says it did to them | A post, quoted | "one user reports", with the link |
| Stated intent | What someone says they will do | A post, quoted | "says they are", never "is" |

Most bad reads of a competitor's change come from moving a line up this table without evidence: a possible impact repeated as though reported, or an intent counted as a departure.

## Is the post about the change?

| The post | Counts as |
| --- | --- |
| Names the change: the plan, the price, the date, the announcement | About the change |
| Describes its direct effect without naming it: "my renewal quote doubled this week" | About the change, marked "inferred from timing and content" |
| Answers a post it does not show, at the time of the change and on its subject | About the change, marked "inferred from timing and content" |
| Complains about the same area with no sign it is new: "it has always been too expensive" | Same topic, change not named |
| Is on the same topic and gives another reason, or none: "the answers got worse, so we are moving off it" | Same topic, change not named |
| Is about the competitor but another matter | Not about the change |
| Predates the change, earlier on the day of the change included | Read for topics, not sorted |

When in doubt, it is "same topic, change not named". Widening the first row is how a quiet reaction becomes a loud one on paper.

Inferred is a test, not a place for what is left over. The post has to describe what this change does: the new price on a bill, the removed plan missing at checkout, the limit hit. Timing alone does not pass it: a post about leaving, written after a price change, that blames something else or gives no reason is same topic.

The mark stays on an inferred post wherever it is listed, and the count is given in two parts: "11 about the change: 7 name it, 4 inferred from timing and content". The same-topic count is given beside it. Same-topic posts are not reaction: they are counted, not sorted into the groups, and not quoted as what the change did.

## The five groups

Each post about the change goes in one group, by what the author says about themselves.

| Group | Test | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reported experience | States a first-hand effect or use, with a detail. Favorable or unfavorable: the sentiment is given with the post | "Our bill went from $400 to $700 with no notice." "The new plan cut our bill by a third." |
| Stated intent | Says they are leaving, have left, are evaluating, or asks for an alternative | "We're moving the team off it this quarter. What are people using instead?" |
| Question | Tries to work out what applies to them | "Does this affect existing annual plans?" |
| Opinion or prediction | No first-hand effect stated, and no approval | "This is going to drive everyone away." |
| Unaffected or in favor | No first-hand effect stated: says it does not touch them, or approves of it | "We're on annual, so nothing changes for us." "Good move, the old plans were confusing." |

Borderline cases:

- "I tried it and it works well" is reported experience, favorable. The same praise from someone who does not say they used it is in favor
- experience and intent in one post: group it as stated intent, and quote the experience too
- "I cancelled" is stated intent already acted on. Say so; it is still the author's word
- a post by someone who says they work for a rival: opinion, and say who they say they are
- a reply that only agrees ("same here") counts as a post in the thread's group, and is not quoted
- sarcasm and jokes: opinion

## Switching language

Posts worth reading closely for Step 7 say one of these things: that they are leaving or have left, that they are evaluating, that they want a recommendation, that a named alternative is better for a stated reason, or that a deadline is forcing a choice.

From each, record what they need (a price ceiling, a feature, a limit, a way to move their data), what they rule out, and which products are named in the post and its replies. A product named in a reply is somebody's suggestion, not the author's choice.

## The two periods

The useful comparison is the same length of time before the change, collected the same way. So the first thing to find is when collection began.

### When collection began

The counts say when social listening was first turned on for the product, and the day each platform's first post was recorded. Take the latest of those days among the platforms that each carry at least a tenth of the after period's posts. A platform under a tenth is left out, so that a few posts from a platform added last week do not cut the read short.

The collection start is a time, not a day: 00:00 UTC on that day, or the time listening was turned on when that is later. Listening turned on at 22:40 starts collection at 22:40, not at the midnight before. With no posts in the after period, it is the time listening was turned on.

Write "collection for this read began on 2025-03-03", and "social listening began on 2025-03-03" when listening being turned on is what set it. Never "monitored since" or "tracked since": the date is when posts began to be collected, and the competitor can have been tracked for much longer.

The first day from the collection start can be a part day of collection. When the start is a time, its hours are known: "80 minutes on the first day: 6 posts". When the start is 00:00 on a platform's first day, the platform began at some hour of that day that the counts do not give: report the day's count on its own as the first day of collection. Either way it stays out of the per-day average.

A period that begins before the collection start was collected on fewer platforms, or on none. Set beside a later one it shows a rise that is only collection starting up. The days need not be empty for this: a thin stretch while collection starts looks like a quiet competitor.

The collection start is the product's. One competitor's keywords can start matching a platform later, and no date records that. So the two periods' splits by platform are compared too, for the platforms that carry at least a tenth of the after period's posts, the same threshold as the collection start. One of those with posts after and none before means the periods do not cover the same ground for this competitor. The count is then given for each period with no per-day pair, and the platform is named with its count in each period. A platform under a tenth does not trip the check: a handful of posts on a new platform leaves a fair comparison fair.

### Posts dated before collection began

The record can hold posts dated before the collection start. They were picked up afterwards, so the days they fall on are not a full count.

- they are in neither period's count and never enter a comparison
- their number goes on a line of its own: "dated before collection began: 14, not a full count". It comes from a count of the stretch between where a period would have begun and the collection start
- when the change itself falls before the collection start, they are read as well: they may be the only posts from the change's first days. Read them as a stretch of their own, under the same page ceiling, with their own "read N of M"
- one that is about the change is sorted like the others and marked "dated before collection began"
- when the change falls after the collection start, they are older than any before period and are not read

### Setting them

After runs from 00:00 UTC on the day of the change to the time of the read, not to the last midnight: a period that stops at midnight leaves out the day of the read. Before is the same length and ends where After begins. When the record gives the change a time of day, the After count also holds the posts from earlier that day. They predate the change and are not sorted as reactions.

| The case | After | Before |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Change 12 days ago, collection began months ago | From 00:00 UTC on the day of the change to the read: 12 whole days and the part day of the read | The same length, ending where After begins |
| Change 45 days ago | Its first 30 days | The 30 days ending where After begins |
| Change 75 days ago | Its first 15 days in the table; its first 30 are read | The 15 days before: posts reach back 90 days, so both are shortened |
| Change more than 90 days ago | Cannot be read | Cannot be read |
| Change 12 days ago, collection began 20 days ago | Its first 8 days in the table; all 12 days and the part day of the read are read | The 8 days from the collection start: both are shortened |
| Change 12 days ago, collection began 5 days ago | From the collection start to the read, said as such. Posts dated in the 7 days before it: their own line, and read | Empty. Nothing is called a rise or a fall |
| Change with no date of its own | The posts since the earliest date known | No column |

### What is compared and what is read

Shortening is for the comparison. When Before cannot be as long as After, both are cut to an equal length for the table, and After keeps its first days: the days nearest the change, set against the days just before it.

The read does not shrink with the table. It runs from where After begins, which is the change or the collection start, to the time of the read, at most 30 days. Cut the read as well and the newest days go unread.

So the table's After and the stretch read can differ. When they do, state both, each with its start and end: "table: the 8 days from the change against the 8 before; read: the 12 days since the change". Every M in "read N of M" is then the count for the whole stretch read, which is the first count, not the table's shortened one. Posts from the days past the table's After are sorted like the rest.

### The change date

The change date is the earliest one a source supports, named with its source. A `published` date is when a source published, and that source can be a commentator writing days after the change: posts between the real date and theirs would be counted as before.

- a change made in stages has several dates: announced, enforced, old plans removed. List them with their sources and split on the earliest. A later stage may be the one the user named, but the reaction starts at the first
- an incident disclosed on one date and written up or analyzed later is one change, dated by its disclosure. The later report is a source, not a stage. Any before and after hangs on the disclosure: split on the report and the before period holds the first of the reaction
- when that later report is what the user asked about, answer about it as well. Count and read the days from the report to the time of the read as a period of its own, headed "since the report". It begins at 00:00 UTC on the day of the report and never before the collection start. It has its own count and its own "read N of M", under the same page ceiling. It has no Before of its own, and nothing is called a rise or a fall for it: the days before the report already hold reaction to the disclosure. Say when the disclosure was and that the report is a later source on it. A post read for this period and for the stretch from the disclosure is one post
- when the user's date differs from the record's, give both, use the record's and say so. The user's date is an account; the record's has a source
- a change known only by when LMTY first observed it, or only through third parties commenting on it, has no date of its own. Any date in hand is later than the change by an unknown time, so a "before" would hold part of the reaction. There is no before and after: report the posts since the earliest such date, with no Before column

A source may also give a second date, when the change takes effect. Count from the change date, and say when the effective date falls: reported experience mostly starts there.

## Counts for one competitor

The counts are taken narrowed to the competitor, once per period: total posts, distinct authors, the daily series, and the split by sentiment, by platform and by tag. They come before any post is read, and they are the "of how many" in every "read N of M".

What a count is:

- a count of keyword matches, and a floor: `SOCIAL.md` says why under "Tags, sentiment, relevance and matched keywords"
- a count of posts, of every kind. How many are about the change is known only for the posts read

A day missing from the daily series had no counted posts. That alone is not silence: before the collection start the count is not a full one, and a later pause in listening leaves no mark.

The answer names the competitor it counted for. When it does not, it counted the whole product, and none of its figures is the competitor's.

| You have | You can say |
| --- | --- |
| Both periods, 20 posts or more in each | "412 posts after, against 180 before. Over the 13 whole days of each: 30 a day against 13. The 9 hours on the day of the read: 22 posts". The two numbers first; a percentage may follow |
| Both periods, fewer than 20 in either | "19 posts in the 14 days after, against 4 in the 14 days before". The two numbers, no percentage |
| Only the period after | "19 posts in the 14 days after; no earlier period could be read". No rise, no fall |
| Shortened periods | "both periods cut to 15 days: posts reach back 90 days", or "both cut to 8 days: collection began on 3 March" |
| A platform with a tenth or more of the posts after and none before | "58 posts after, against 12 before. No per-day figures: [platform] has 40 of the 58 after and none before, so the two periods do not cover the same ground". No rise, no fall |
| A platform under a tenth of the posts after and none before | The comparison stands: "3 of the 900 posts after are on [platform], which has none before" |
| Posts dated before the collection start | "dated before collection began: 14, not a full count", on its own line and in neither column |
| Thin days inside a period | "41 a day after against 52 before. 4 days after are under a quarter of the median day and may be a gap in collection: without them, 55 against 52. The direction changes with them, so this is neither a rise nor a fall" |
| A later report the user asked about | "since the report, 6 days: 310 posts, read 94. The disclosure was 12 days earlier; the report is a later source on it. No earlier period is set against these days" |

A per-day average is taken from the daily series, over whole UTC days, and says how many. The day of the read is a part day, and so is the first day of a before period of the same length: each is a count with its hours, never folded into the average. So is the first day from the collection start. Collection can also lag: when the newest post received is hours older than the time of the read, the day of the read holds fewer hours of posts than the clock says. The number of days the counts give for a period is rounded up, so dividing by it gives an average that is too low.

### A thin stretch inside a period

A day can be present in the daily series and still be thin: collection slowed or paused, and nothing marks it. A few such days at one end of a period move its average enough to make a rise or a fall.

- in each compared period, take the median of its whole days, a day absent from the series counting as zero
- name every whole day under a quarter of that median, with its count, as a possible gap in collection. A possible gap, not a known one: a quiet day looks the same
- give the per-day figures twice, with those days and without them
- if the direction of the difference is the same both ways, the usual rules apply. If it changes, say only that it changes, and call nothing a rise or a fall

Part days are not tested: they are in no average.

More posts after a change are not shown to be because of it. A launch, an outage or one long thread in the same days moves the count as well. Report the two numbers and what the posts read say.

## Reading the posts

Counts say how much. Only reading says what about.

- the stretch from where After begins to the time of the read, at most 30 days, narrowed to the competitor, newest first and untagged: up to 5 pages, or at most 2 when the stretch holds more posts than 5 pages can return
- when those pages end before the period does, by tag as well, with the same narrowing and at most 5 pages per tag
- one untagged page of the before period, for what people talked about on ordinary days, and only when Before can be compared

Five pages is the ceiling per filter for the whole period. Cutting a period in two to read five more pages of each half is the same filter read past its ceiling.

Pages run newest first: 5 pages of a period that holds thousands of posts are a few hours of its last day, not the period. That is why a busy competitor gets 2 untagged pages, as a sample of what an unselected hour looks like, and the tags do the rest. A read by tag leans toward what the tags select: pages of posts tagged leaving find people leaving. Say so.

Which tags depends on the change. The after period's split by tag shows where the posts are:

| The change | Tags that bear on it |
| --- | --- |
| A price or plan change | Pricing and leaving (churn intent), then user feedback and bug reports. These are the default four, for a price change only |
| An incident or outage | Bug reports and user feedback |
| A launch | Launch announcements, product questions and user feedback |

Read the ones with the most posts first, and none with no posts. Promotional, directory, first-party and machine-written tags are never read for a reaction.

Say "read N of M" for each filter, with the days or hours its pages covered.

- N is counted from the posts received, never taken from the page size asked for
- M is the count for the same filters over the stretch read: its total for untagged pages, its split by tag for a tag
- a read narrowed further, by sentiment as well as a tag, has no M when the counts cannot take that filter: "28 read, total not known"
- a post read under two filters is one post

On each page, check that the posts carry one of the competitor's phrases among their matched keywords. When they do not, or the counts did not name the competitor, the connection cannot narrow to it. Then read by tag without the narrowing, keep the posts whose matched keywords name the competitor, and count only what was read: both columns of period counts stay empty, nothing is called a rise or a fall, and a line says which tags were read, the days their pages covered, and that untagged posts were not read.

## Authors

`SOCIAL.md` says who an author is, under "Authors". In this read:

- quote with the handle; add the display name and followers when present
- never rank posts by followers alone. A large account shows reach, not weight of evidence
- never call a large account representative, and never call an author a customer

## Sizing

Give five numbers: posts that matched the competitor in the period, posts read, posts about the change (those that name it and those inferred, apart), distinct authors, platforms. The same-topic count stands beside them. The number dated before collection began is on its own line, outside both periods' counts.

Distinct authors appear twice and are kept apart. For a period, the figure comes from the counts: distinct handles per platform, among posts that have one. For the posts about the change, count the handles on the posts read.

Then check for concentration. If one thread holds more than half of the posts about the change, say so and give the count without it. A thread with 300 replies is one conversation.

Engagement counts (votes, likes, comments) show how far a post reached. They are not agreement and are never added to the post count.

## What the other records can add

A product's public profile may show customers whose use ended after the change, and monthly counts of posts about buying and leaving.

- an ended customer after the change is worth naming, with how the end is known. It is not shown to be because of the change unless a source says so
- a month's count covers the whole month. A change on the 27th explains little of that month's number
- these records lag by weeks

## What not to write

| Do not write | Because | Write |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Customers are leaving Northwind." | Posts are not customers, and intent is not departure | "7 posts say the author is leaving or evaluating alternatives." |
| "Backlash over the price increase." | A word for scale with no number | "31 posts matched Northwind in the 14 days after, against 6 in the 14 days before. 19 of the 31 are about the change." |
| "Mentions tripled because of the change." | A percentage on a small count, and a cause | "18 posts in the 14 days after, against 6 before." |
| "A founder with 80,000 followers speaks for the market." | Reach is not representation | "One post is from an account with 80,000 followers; it is one post." |
| "The change is driving churn." | Cause, and a metric nobody published | "No source reports churn figures. 3 known customers are marked ended since." |
| "Users hate the new limits." | One group standing for all | "12 posts report hitting the new limit; 4 say it does not affect them." |
| "This is a big opening for us." | A decision, and a forecast | "5 of the 7 looking for an alternative name a price ceiling under $20 a seat." |

## Replies and outreach

The skill finds conversations. It does not join them. When the user asks for a reply or an opener, write a draft: two or three sentences, specific to what the author said, no pitch, nothing about the competitor that is not sourced. Mark it for a person to review and send.

## Length

The tables carry the read. Outside them, under 500 words. On a day with nothing new, one line. With few or no posts, the short form: the counts for each period, how many posts were read over which days, and the "Not observed" section, with the other sections left out.
